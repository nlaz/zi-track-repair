What was actually wrong

The skipping was real. The clipping was not.

The report of “skipping and clipping artifacts” turned out to be one defect, misattributed. There were 241 genuine dropouts — the stream cutting out for a median of 18 ms at a time, 3.7 times a minute, getting worse as the session ran on.

But there was no clipping anywhere in the file: peak level −0.32 dBFS, zero full-scale samples, zero flat-topped waveforms at any level, and a true peak of −0.29 dBTP with no inter-sample overs. What sounded like clipping was the transient at each dropout's edge — a 45 dB collapse inside 2 ms produces a click that reads as distortion by ear. Chasing a limiter would have found nothing.

How each hole was filled

Two methods, split at 25 ms

Nothing here recovers lost data — it never arrived and there was no redundancy to rebuild it from. Both channels were more than 20 dB down in 235 of the 241 events, so even cross-channel reconstruction was impossible. What the repair does is invent audio convincing enough that the seam disappears.

Which method wins depends on gap length, and that crossover was measured rather than assumed: a clean passage was cut open, filled by each method, and scored against the audio deliberately removed.

Rather than pick a method from gap length alone, every gap now gets eight candidate fills — the six best-matching passages from elsewhere in the track, a pitch-synchronous repeat of its own neighbours, and an AR interpolation — and each is scored on how closely its energy matches the surrounding music band by band. The best-scoring candidate wins. In practice that chooses a real passage from elsewhere in the set 230 times and a pitch-synchronous repeat 8 times; autoregressive interpolation never wins outright, which is the correct outcome given the table above.

Every fill is then matched to the local high-frequency ceiling, crossfaded 10 ms into the measured audio at both ends rather than hard-spliced, and capped so it can never exceed the original's peak.

Repairs are wider than the silences. These dropouts don't stop and restart — the stream cuts out in under 2 ms, then fades back in over 30–60 ms. That ramp is damaged audio too: real music at the wrong, rising level. Repairing only the silent core leaves it behind and the result swells into place. Spans were extended to cover the cliff, the floor and the whole ramp, which is why 11.59 s was replaced to fix 6.17 s of silence.

Seam figures compare each repair boundary's sample step against the 99.5th percentile of its neighbours. A control of random points in untouched audio scored a median of 0.22 — so the repaired seams are, by this measure, as smooth as naturally occurring transitions. None exceeds 2×.

Two of these checks exist because an earlier version of this repair passed the others and was still wrong. Fill level catches a fill that decays quietly to nothing — a hole with no sharp edge for a dropout detector to find. Broadband splice burst measures energy from 19–23 kHz, where this material has no content at all, so any hard splice shows up there unmistakably even when the music masks it lower down. Both failures were visible in the spectrogram before any metric caught them.

The repaired master was produced losslessly; this MP3 is a 192 kbps encode of it, matching the original's format so the two are directly comparable. Ask if you need the lossless file — it is too large for this repository.

Honest caveats. The 230 matched fills are real audio lifted from elsewhere in the set. They are musically plausible and spectrally continuous, but they are not what was played in those moments. For a commercial release, spot-check the longest by ear — 26:45.399, 44:11.734, 41:53.245 and 12:02.938 — and remember that a re-capture from the performer's own local recording would still beat all of this.

One span resists a good fill: 26:45.658, 20 ms, sits 12.7 dB below its neighbours because no comparable passage exists nearby. It is the single worst fill in the track and the only one more than 10 dB down.
